B. What the NIH research lets us say

The 2018 study (Nature, NIH-led): 200 people in rural Thailand. None of the people with Bacillus in their gut carried staph. In mice, B. subtilis given every other day cleared staph from the gut. It works by blocking the signals staph uses to coordinate (fengycins interfere with quorum sensing). In people this was an observation; the cause-and-effect was shown in mice.

The 2023 trial (The Lancet Microbe, phase 2, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, funded in part by the NIH): 115 adults in Thailand who already carried staph took B. subtilis MB40 (250 mg once a day) or a placebo for 30 days. Staph in the gut fell 96.8% and in the nose 65.4% compared with placebo, with no difference in side effects. It measured colonization, not infections.

We can say:

  • Staph Aid is built around published research on spore probiotics and staph (true if the formula was developed from it).
  • A factual description of the studies, with the citation next to it: who, how many, how long, what changed, and that they measured colonization, not infections.
  • Structure and function wording with the FDA disclaimer: “formulated to support skin, gut and immune health*”, “Vitamin C supports normal immune function*”.
  • “Spore probiotic”, “17 billion CFU”, and “third-party tested” once the lab report exists.

We can’t say:

  • “Designed to fight Staph and MRSA”, “prevents infection”, “kills staph”. Staph and MRSA infections are diseases, and a supplement can’t claim to prevent or treat a disease. It doesn’t matter that it’s technically what the formula was designed around: the FDA and the ad platforms judge the words, not the intent.
  • “Staph Aid reduces staph”, or the trial’s 96.8% / 65.4% next to our product, unless Staph Aid uses the trial’s strain (MB40) at its dose (250 mg a day). The FTC expects evidence on the same ingredient, strain and dose. Even with a match, “reduces staph colonization” sits close to a disease claim and needs a lawyer’s sign-off.
  • “Clinically proven”, “clinically tested”: there is no trial on Staph Aid itself.
  • “World’s first”: a “first” claim needs proof that nobody did it earlier, which is hard to prove and easy to challenge.

Brad’s other question was whether it hurts to hide the ingredient name. It doesn’t hurt the claims. The one place the name has to appear is wherever the research is described, because the research is about that ingredient. Before any paid ads run, have a lawyer who knows FDA and FTC supplement rules read the product page, the Science page and the ads. This guide is a copywriter’s read, not legal advice.

  2. Strain and dose: does Staph Aid use B. subtilis MB40 at 250 mg a day, like the trial? This decides how much of the research we can put next to the product.

  6. Testimonials: the three rewritten reviews are placeholders. Rewording a real customer’s review is a fake review under the FTC’s 2024 rule. Before launch, pick real reviews that don’t make disease claims and quote them word for word.
  7. Press ticker: left as it is. I do recommend one caption line under it: “Headlines about the research on spore probiotics, not reviews of Staph Aid.” Without it, the quotes read as coverage of our product. The Science and How it works pages already carry that line.

  9. Clinician reviews on the product page are treated as our own claims (“prevent them from colonizing”, “eliminating the internal reservoir”). They need the same legal pass, and one of them mentions prebiotic fibers, which aren’t in the formula.
  10. Free shipping: the FAQ says free on US orders over $25, but a single refill is $24.99.

The original recommendations (Sept 27, before round 4):

One theme runs through the whole list: copy that ads can’t run is copy that can’t scale. Google, Meta and payment processors reject disease claims (“fights MRSA”, “deadly”, “clinically proven”). Every rewrite here keeps the staph story, which is why people buy, and stays inside “formulated to support”.

1. The ten changes that matter most

  1. HighOne offer, said the same way everywhere

    Visitors see “Save up to 70%” (banner, hero, tiles), “60% off” (popup and footer), “Save 16%” (buy box) and a $49.99 “regular” price. Mixed numbers make people stop and wonder which one is real, and some leave. Lead everywhere with “Up to 70% off + a FREE $70 Starter Kit”. Make the popup an extra first-order code (for example “extra 10% off”) instead of a competing 60%.

  2. HighMake the guarantee say one thing

    The banner promises a refund “if you don’t experience noticeable improvements within 30 days, no questions asked”. The FAQ says unopened packs only, minus $5 return shipping. A buyer who opens a bag and asks for a refund will feel misled, and that turns into chargebacks and bad reviews. Choose one policy and use the same words everywhere. If the numbers allow it, “30 days, even if the bag is open” is the version that sells.

  3. HighOnly proof we can stand behind

    The testing panel (70 pesticides / 4 / 16 / 9) is Grüns’ own data, not ours. “15,732 reviews” and “100,000+ orders” can’t be confirmed from the store’s analytics. “Clinically proven results” sits over placeholder numbers. Borrowed or unverifiable proof is the fastest way to lose a skeptical buyer, and a legal risk. Swap in our lab results, our real review count, and research wording we can cite.

  4. HighPromises that ads can run

    “Designed to fight Staph and MRSA”, “deadly”, “clinically proven” and “reduced inflammation” are disease claims. Google and Meta ad review, payment processors and the FTC all flag this kind of language. Copy that can’t be advertised can’t scale. The rewrites below stay inside “formulated to support” and keep the staph angle as the reason people care.

  5. MediumSpeak to everyone staph touches

    Staph isn’t a niche problem: about 30% of people carry it, and anyone can get an infection. The site should say so plainly and then name the people who worry about it most: families, older adults and the people who care for them, nurses and healthcare workers, people with skin infections or boils that keep coming back, and athletes. Add a “Made for…” strip that links each group to its blog topic, and add a family option to the quiz popup. People buy faster when they see themselves in the copy.

  6. MediumButtons that say what happens

    “Start Now” and “Learn More” don’t tell the buyer anything. Buttons that show the price and the gift win more clicks: “Start My Refills: $49.98 + FREE Kit”.

  7. MediumUse the words people search for

    The biggest searches are everyday questions: “staph infection”, “MRSA”, “is staph contagious”, “what does staph look like”. Google hides their volume in Keyword Planner, but they dwarf everything else. Those belong to the blog’s pillar guide, linked from the homepage and Science page. On the product page, use the product words people search for: “bacillus subtilis probiotic” (~2,900 a month) and “spore based probiotics” (~2,400), not only “Staph Aid Probiotic Capsules”.

  8. MediumTestimonials that sell without making claims

    The FTC treats reviews shown on the site as the brand’s own claims. “Haven’t been sick in months” and “finally broke the cycle… antibiotics” are disease claims. Lead with reviews about the habit, the confidence and the ease, add who the reviewer is (sport, job, months using it), and keep “results vary”.

  9. LowTell the founder story

    Our Story opens with “We’re a brand focused on the health and wellness of our community”, which could be any company. Why did Brad start Staph Aid? A true, specific origin story is one of the cheapest trust builders a small brand has.

  10. LowFix the typos

    “Bacillus Subtilus”, “were show to”, “at least 3 month”, “3/4 days”, “everyday” (as an adverb), “Try Now-Risk Free”, “3rd Party Clinically Tested”. Small errors on a health product read as carelessness.

8. What to test first

Once the Shopify store is live and there is enough traffic, run these as A/B tests. A test settles what opinions can’t.

  1. Hero headline: Brad’s “Staph is everywhere. Protection is here.” vs. “The Daily Probiotic Built Around the Staph Research.”
  2. Guarantee: “even if the bag is open” vs. “unopened bags”. Watch refunds as well as conversions.
  3. Offer framing: “Up to 70% off” vs. “Save $174.98 + FREE Kit” on the Buy 3, Get 2 tile.
  4. Main button: “Start Now” vs. “Start My Refills: $49.98 + FREE Kit”.
