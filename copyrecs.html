For Brad · copywriting review

Copy recommendations for sales and ROI

The design is in good shape. The words are now the biggest lever on sales. This is a full read of every page as a direct-response copywriter would do it, using the new search data. Nothing has been changed yet. Pick what you like and we’ll apply it in one pass.

One theme runs through the whole list: copy that ads can’t run is copy that can’t scale. Google, Meta and payment processors reject disease claims (“fights MRSA”, “deadly”, “clinically proven”). Every rewrite here keeps the staph story, which is why people buy, and stays inside “formulated to support”.

1. The ten changes that matter most

  1. HighOne offer, said the same way everywhere

    Visitors see “Save up to 70%” (banner, hero, tiles), “60% off” (popup and footer), “Save 16%” (buy box) and a $49.99 “regular” price. Mixed numbers make people stop and wonder which one is real, and some leave. Lead everywhere with “Up to 70% off + a FREE $70 Starter Kit”. Make the popup an extra first-order code (for example “extra 10% off”) instead of a competing 60%.

  2. HighMake the guarantee say one thing

    The banner promises a refund “if you don’t experience noticeable improvements within 30 days, no questions asked”. The FAQ says unopened packs only, minus $5 return shipping. A buyer who opens a bag and asks for a refund will feel misled, and that turns into chargebacks and bad reviews. Choose one policy and use the same words everywhere. If the numbers allow it, “30 days, even if the bag is open” is the version that sells.

  3. HighOnly proof we can stand behind

    The testing panel (70 pesticides / 4 / 16 / 9) is Grüns’ own data, not ours. “15,732 reviews” and “100,000+ orders” can’t be confirmed from the store’s analytics. “Clinically proven results” sits over placeholder numbers. Borrowed or unverifiable proof is the fastest way to lose a skeptical buyer, and a legal risk. Swap in our lab results, our real review count, and research wording we can cite.

  4. HighPromises that ads can run

    “Designed to fight Staph and MRSA”, “deadly”, “clinically proven” and “reduced inflammation” are disease claims. Google and Meta ad review, payment processors and the FTC all flag this kind of language. Copy that can’t be advertised can’t scale. The rewrites below stay inside “formulated to support” and keep the staph angle as the reason people care.

  5. MediumName the three buyers

    The quiz popup already knows who buys: people who train on mats or at the gym, people who work in healthcare, and people who care for someone at higher risk. The search data adds a fourth: people with boils that keep coming back. Say this on the homepage in a “Made for…” strip that links to the matching blog topic. People buy faster when they see themselves in the copy.

  6. MediumButtons that say what happens

    “Start Now” and “Learn More” don’t tell the buyer anything. Buttons that show the price and the gift win more clicks: “Start My Refills: $49.98 + FREE Kit”.

  7. MediumUse the words people search for

    People search “bacillus subtilis probiotic” (~2,900 a month) and “spore based probiotics” (~2,400), not “Staph Aid Probiotic Capsules”. Put those words in page titles, the product headline and the first paragraph. Link the product page to the boils and wrestling articles, where the biggest search volume is.

  8. MediumTestimonials that sell without making claims

    The FTC treats reviews shown on the site as the brand’s own claims. “Haven’t been sick in months” and “finally broke the cycle… antibiotics” are disease claims. Lead with reviews about the habit, the confidence and the ease, add who the reviewer is (sport, job, months using it), and keep “results vary”.

  9. LowTell the founder story

    Our Story opens with “We’re a brand focused on the health and wellness of our community”, which could be any company. Why did Brad start Staph Aid? A true, specific origin story is one of the cheapest trust builders a small brand has.

  10. LowFix the typos

    “Bacillus Subtilus”, “were show to”, “at least 3 month”, “3/4 days”, “everyday” (as an adverb), “Try Now-Risk Free”, “3rd Party Clinically Tested”. Small errors on a health product read as carelessness.

8. What to test first

Once the Shopify store is live and there is enough traffic, run these as A/B tests. A test settles what opinions can’t.

  1. Hero headline: Brad’s “Staph is everywhere. Protection is here.” vs. “The Daily Probiotic Built Around the Staph Research.”
  2. Guarantee: “even if the bag is open” vs. “unopened bags”. Watch refunds as well as conversions.
  3. Offer framing: “Up to 70% off” vs. “Save $174.98 + FREE Kit” on the Buy 3, Get 2 tile.
  4. Main button: “Start Now” vs. “Start My Refills: $49.98 + FREE Kit”.
