How the blog can bring in customers from Google

The short version: staph affects everyone (families, older adults, nurses, caregivers, athletes), and the searches people make about it are huge. Almost nobody finds staphaid.com through Google today (21 visits from search in nine months). A clinician-reviewed blog that answers the everyday staph and MRSA questions, starting with one complete guide to staph infections, is the cheapest way to reach them.

Why the numbers below undercount the market

Google Keyword Planner withholds search volume for mainstream health terms such as “staph infection”, “MRSA” and “staph infection symptoms”, so the export only shows the long tail. Dan puts “staph infection” alone at roughly 600,000 searches a month worldwide. Treat everything Keyword Planner shows as the floor, not the market. To size the mainstream terms we need a third-party tool such as Semrush or Ahrefs (both have free trials), and later the site’s own Search Console data.

1. Where the site stands now

  • 638 visits in nine months (about 70 a month), and roughly a third look like bots.
  • Google search sent 21 visits. Google Shopping’s free listings sent 36, and those visitors stayed the longest.
  • Google Analytics records no purchases at all. Either sales happen somewhere else or the tracking is broken. That needs fixing before we can show the blog made money.
  • Search Console is already set up for staphaid.com. Whoever owns it needs to add Dan so we can see the search data.

2. The plan: one big guide, then answers for everyone

  1. A pillar guide to staph infections: what it is, who gets it, what it looks like, how it spreads, when to see a doctor, how to lower your risk. It targets the mainstream searches and is written and in the prototype now.
  2. Answers to the everyday questions underneath it: is staph contagious, how you get it, what it looks like, staph vs. MRSA, how long it lives on surfaces, staph in your nose.
  3. A section for each group staph touches: families and kids, older adults and caregivers, nurses and healthcare workers, people with recurring skin infections, and athletes. Each is a topic in the blog menu.
  4. Every article links back to the pillar and to the product, so the authority the blog builds flows to the pages that sell.

3. What’s realistic

  • The exact phrase “staph infection” is owned by the CDC, Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic and WebMD. A supplement brand is unlikely to reach page one for that phrase in the first year, whatever we write.
  • What we can win are the question searches underneath it, Google’s “People also ask” boxes, and the answers that ChatGPT and Google’s AI summaries give, which cite clear, sourced, clinician-reviewed pages. Together these add up to a large share of the mainstream traffic. Expect 6–12 months of steady publishing.
  • Many mainstream searchers have an active infection and need a doctor, not a supplement. The blog earns their trust and their email. Sales come mostly from readers thinking about prevention, repeat infections, a household member or someone they care for. Measure the blog on email sign-ups and assisted sales, not just same-visit purchases.

6. The rules that keep us out of trouble

  1. No treatment claims. Staph Aid is “formulated to support”. Articles about infections, boils or MRSA tell readers to see a doctor, and never present Staph Aid as a treatment.
  2. Every article is reviewed by a clinician and names them, and every fact links to its source (CDC, NIH, published studies). Google weighs this heavily for health topics.
  3. The herpes searches are for awareness only. Herpes is a virus; Staph Aid is never linked to it.
  4. Legal reviews anything claim-like before it goes live.
